Where the weight is

Hardware

  • One .375" Grade 5 zinc bolt per crossing or T (through both tubes), flange nuts or nylocks. The frame is sized so that is enough; brackets add stiffness, not strength it needs.
  • Straight splices: 12" stub of 1.75" inside the 2" ends, two bolts each side. Keep the 96" sticks whole where you can.
  • Chassis: have a shop weld the 2 rails, 4 cross-members and 4 caster plates as one flat frame from plain 2.5" × 14 ga, then paint. ~1 hr of welding. Bolting it instead works too, with the rails stacked on top of the cross-members and the deck ply holding it square.
  • Casters: four 16" rated ≥ 1,200 lb each, under the rails at 11" from each end. Rigid at the rear, swivel at the front.
  • Deck ply: screw to the steel every 6–8" along every panel edge with #12 self-drillers. The decks are the sideways bracing; this is the one place fastener spacing matters.

Cut list

Physical pieces (model segments merged). Lap-joint overlaps add ~4" to members that run past a corner; cut after you decide which member runs through.

Caster loads, lb

Vertical reaction at each tyre contact. "Lifted" removes the right-rear wheel, as on a crowned road or a pothole; "Stop" brakes the rear wheels at g with the crowd moving.

What it means

  • Buy casters for the worst number, not the parked one: lb per wheel.
  • Each caster is modelled as a stiff leg from the rail down to the road, pinned at the tyre, so a sideways or braking force overturns about the road and its " lever twists the rail and bends the cross-members, as a bolted-on caster plate does. Horizontal restraint is caster friction; the frame is braced so it does not depend on it.
  • Track is " (rails at ±"), wheelbase ".
  • Braking is a pitch problem before it is a strength problem: the body is tall and the crowd sits high, so a hard stop lifts the rear wheels before anything in the frame is near capacity (frame at % in the Stop case). Any brake goes on the rear wheels and should be self-limiting (a tread shoe or a skidding tyre: friction falls as the wheel unloads). A chock the rear tyre climbs is a positive stop at walking pace; at 3 mph it is marginal, because the pitch unloads the wheel just as it climbs. The brake is a to-do; the mechanism is written up in notes/brake.md.
  • With the sides braced, brackets at the post ends change the pothole twist from " to " and the rope-snatch racking from " to ".
  • Rigid casters at the rear, swivels at the front: the float tracks like a wagon instead of crabbing on a crowned road, and a brake knows where the rear tyre is.

Bracket kit

Verticals lap past their horizontals instead of butting them, so both tubes present one shared flat face and a laser-cut flat plate bolts across it: two bolts in each tube, no bends. Every diagonal in the body is 45°, so there are two plate patterns, counted from the model. Diagonals at other angles are flattened-end pins and need none.

The model places members on their centrelines; the 1.5" offset of a lapped post is noise structurally. It is sized with every one of these joints as a pin, so the plates add stiffness and consistency, not strength it needs.

Sticks meeting at each joint

Largest joint forces

One .375" bolt through two 14 ga walls ≈ 3,600 lb in bearing (2.0·d·t·Fu ÷ 2, two walls). Nothing here is above a third of that.

Tightening

  • These are bearing joints: the bolt shank pushes on the hole edge. Clamp force adds almost nothing, so do not torque to spec.
  • Snug-tight (hand wrench, wall just touching) plus thread locker. Where a bolt passes through a single tube with nothing inside it, a 1.75" sleeve stub inside the 2" tube lets you tighten harder without dimpling.
  • Keep ≥ 2 holes (2") from any cut end to the first bolt; edge tear-out, not bolt shear, is the usual failure.

What changed in v8

  • Lower box: a V at every rib from the rail ends up to a 2" deck spine, the deck beams across the apexes. No joists, columns or side diagonals and the deck beams are better off, propped at mid-span by the spine. Rails stay 2.5" (at 2" they go over 100% with a wheel in a pothole); cross-members 2". V's at the end ribs only was tried: 10 lb lighter, but the rails go to 85% and the pothole twist from 0.23" to 0.38".
  • Long braces and the nose X stand on the deck-ring corners, where the chamfer stubs take the load straight to the rail ends.
  • Upper chamfer stubs moved off the hatch-beam stations to Y = ": the hatch beams now meet the side rails alone (plain TP-90), and each stub lands on bare rail (plain TP-45).
  • Three posts per side at Y = ": one on each body corner and one mid-side. The corner posts give the side ply and the end-wall ply a vertical edge to screw to (without them the two sheets met at a bare seam) and carry the upper-deck corners directly; the mid post laps a continuous rail. Each side skin is two 48 × 55 pieces, one per bay, no horizontal seam. The staggered layout (16, 48, 80) is one line away and models the same. No cross piece over the top deck where someone would sit: rim crosses only at the nose and tail. Upper deck joists reduced to the two hatch sides; the ply spans 32" between the side-to-side beams.
  • Casters modelled to the road: each is a stiff leg from the rail to the tyre contact, so sideways and braking forces overturn about the road and the caster's 17" lever twists the rail and bends the cross-members. Rails went 75% → 85% (sideways case), cross-members 6% → 34%. A Stop case (0.3 g held at the rear tyres) was added; see Joints & casters.
  • Cables, if you want them: CABLES=('sides',) adds a tension-only cable X across each side wall, the four upper corners down to the opposite lower corners, with turnbuckles. With the side ply fastened they carry 12–22 lb (the panel is eight times stiffer, so it takes the load); if every side seam slipped and the ply did nothing, racking goes 0.08" → 0.27" and the pothole twist 0.23" → 0.43" with nothing over 85%, and ⅛" cables bring that back to 0.16" / 0.36" at 130 lb of tension. Insurance, not structure; off by default. 'nose' swaps the nose-wall tube X for cables: saves 16 lb, but the nose pyramid is ~80× stiffer than a cable and the cables model at 0 lb. Cables cannot replace the long braces, the V's or the chamfer stubs: those are all in compression in every load case.
  • Fixed: the axial sign in the member checks. PyNite reports compression as positive and the check had it backwards since v1, so compression members were not getting the buckling check and tension members were. Long braces went 5% → 12% (they are props, −250 lb each parked, −485 lb in a pothole); nothing else moved more than 3 points.
  • Tail squared up: a 55 × 55" flap 20.5" behind the body, so every tail face is a 45° cut, same as the octagon chamfers, and the corner skin pieces are all alike. lb of steel.

The load path

  • People → deck ply (spanning 32") → deck beams at the ribs → the V at that rib → chassis rail ends → casters. The upper deck comes down the posts into the deck-edge stringers and beams, then the chamfer stubs carry it to the chassis corners.
  • Sideways racking → deck ply diaphragms → the X-braced nose wall and the skinned tail-wall panels beside the door → chassis.
  • Front-to-back racking (tow snatch, stopping) → the four long braces and the hard side panels, straight down to the chassis. The nose and tail pyramids help; the body is a closed braced tube.
  • Someone sits on a hatch edge → hatch-edge beam → long braces → chassis, instead of the beam spanning 96" on its own.
  • Every joint is a pin, so nothing relies on a bracket, but ideally we'll use brackets anyway since they are what turns "strong enough" into "f-yeah".

Build order

Four to six people, a flat floor, two sawhorses, a level. Nothing gets lifted that two people cannot carry; the heavy moment is setting the chassis on its wheels.

  1. Plates and tube first. Cut every stick to the cut list and label it. Zinc-rich (cold galvanizing) spray on every cut end and every hole you drill, as you go. Flatten the ends of the cone stringers, long braces and V-braces (2" in a vise, drill .375"). Put threadlocker on every bolt as you go; nothing gets re-torqued later.
  2. Chassis, upside down on sawhorses. Two 2.5" rails, four 2" cross-members: rails stacked on the cross-members, one bolt through each crossing, TL-90 plates at the four corners. Square it by diagonals before the plates go on. Bolt the four caster plates to the underside of the rails with the crush sleeves inside the rail. Fit the belly ply (two sheets, " × 96") to the underside now, while it is face up and easy. Consider running a bead of silicon down each steel peice first and letting it dry to see if it reduces squeaking.
  3. Flip it onto its wheels. Four people, lift and roll. **Chock the wheels**. Everything from here is built on the chassis at working height, " off the floor.
  4. Lower box. At each rib: a V of two 1.5" diagonals from the rail ends up to the deck centre (flattened-end pins), the 96" deck beam across their apex, and the two chamfer stubs from the rail ends out to the deck-ring corners (TP-45). Then the 2" deck spine along the four apexes and the deck-edge stringers along the corners. Every rib is a triangle on the rails; check it is square and level before the ply.
  5. Lower deck ply. Two 4 × 8 sheets dropped in from above, long way across the beams. Screw to every beam and stringer every 6–8". This is the first diaphragm; the body gets stiff when it goes down.
  6. Posts and upper frame. Six posts: four on the body corners (TP-90 to the deck-edge stringer and the side rail, outboard face), two mid-side (TP-90 top and bottom). Then the end beams and the two hatch-edge beams across (TP-90 to the rails), the hatch sides, the nose-wall X-brace, the tail-door jambs.
  7. Long braces. Four of them, standing on the deck-ring corners of the end ribs (over the chamfer stubs, bolt through the ply into the beam end) and rising to the hatch-edge beams: front pair to ±" on the Y=64 beam, rear pair to ±" on the Y=32 beam. Pinned both ends. Their load goes brace → corner → chamfer stub → rail end, never into a beam mid-span.
  8. Upper deck ply through the top. The top opening is " wide, a 4 × 8 sheet is 48", so the sheets go in from above, long way across the beams, with the hatch cut out. Screw every 6–8".
  9. Rim. Chamfer stubs (TP-45) at Y = ", so the inner pair sit on plain rail between the posts and the hatch beams, then the two rim stringers, cross pieces at the nose and anus only.
  10. Nose and tail frames. Snout and tail frames assembled flat on the floor (TL-90 corners), then the eight stringers each, pinned at both ends. Hang the anal door.
  11. Skin, outside in, bottom up. Lower chamfers, sides (two 48 × 55 pieces per side, each cut from one 4 × 8 sheet; the 48 × 41 offcuts skin the chamfer faces), upper chamfers, cones. Every panel fastened along all four edges, #12 self-drillers every 6–8": the side and chamfer panels are bracing, not decoration, and a sheet edge with nothing behind it is a seam that will work loose. Seal the ply edges before they go on. If you add the side cables, fit them before the side ply: eyebolts through the corner tubes with a 1.5" plate washer inside, turnbuckles snug plus half a turn, no more. Use cardboard to measure and cut the "legs" out of goldenrod coroplast, roll them into circles, staple and glue, thn add the rubber garage door trim to the bottom and attach them to the body with l-brackets.
  12. Attach rope, attach two large tow points and D-Rings to the front under the snout, right into the 2" crossmember. Consider adding truck nutz to the rear (not for towing).
  13. Finish. Solvent wipe, self-etching or galvanized-metal primer, then the goldenrod. Cavity wax (Fluid Film, Woolwax, 3M Cavity Wax) through the perforations with the 360° wand after paint, never before: wax under paint lifts it. Plastic caps on the open tube ends; the perforations drain the rest. Attach a goldenrod pool noodle to a curled piece of electrical conduit and affix it through the tail hole above the anal door. Add goldenrod pool noodles to all interior metal framing, twist with raquet grip everywhere you'll grab. Install two motion sensing LED battery-powered lights to the bottom of each half of the upper deck.

The busiest joint

The two lower nose corners carry seven sticks: deck beam end, edge-stringer end, corner post, chamfer stub, nose stringer, long brace and X-brace. Build them in that order: TL-90 on the beam/stringer corner, TP-90 for the post on the stringer's outboard face, TP-45 for the stub under the corner, then the three flattened-end pins staggered one hole apart along the beam end. The tail corners are the same less the X-brace. The chassis corners are five: rail end, cross-member end, chamfer stub, V-brace, cone stringer. Interior rail nodes are three: rail through, cross-member end, V-brace pin.

Plates

Flat, 10 ga (0.135") mild steel, zinc plated. Slots instead of holes so the plate lands on the tube's 1" hole pitch at any phase. Two plates per joint, one each face; one bolt passes plate–tube–plate at every slot. DXF files are in the repo at docs/exports/plates/.

Bolts and nuts

All .375"-16 Grade 5 zinc hex bolts with serrated flange nuts, in total; order the next box size up. Lengths assume 10 ga plates both sides and 14 ga tube. Where there is no plate, a washer sized to the tube face: 1.25" on 1.5" tube, 1.5" on 1.75", 2" on the 2" and 2.5".

Torque

The rated torque for a .375" Grade 5 is about 31 ft·lb, which puts 4,500 lb of clamp on a 0.075" wall and dimples it. These are bearing joints with threadlocker, so the nut only has to stay put. Set a torque wrench and stop there.

Loads

  • Steel self-weight: 2.5" , 2" , 1.75" , 1.5" lb/ft (14 ga).
  • Skin + paint + decoration psf on every face including both cones, top open (.75" marine ply is 2.3 psf, so it fits). Deck ply psf (.75").
  • Parked: people standing below ( lb each, spread) plus one lb person sitting at the centre of each hatch edge. Six aboard.
  • Moving: one lb person mid-deck below and one lb person at a hatch edge (you said one; this is two). Road shock is × that.
  • Sway: g sideways, parked crowd. Tow: × moving weight snatched at two eyes on the front rail ends, rope 20° up, with matching inertia.
  • Lifted: right-rear caster off the ground, parked crowd.
  • Stop: moving crowd, g forward inertia held at the rear tyres only (front casters free to roll): a chock or tread brake on the rear wheels.

Model

  • PyNite 3D frame, 1D beam elements. Two tubes crossing at 90° share one bolt, so every such joint is modelled as a pin: posts, chamfer stubs, cone stringers, V-braces and long braces at both ends; deck beams, rails, joists and X-braces at their ends. Moment exists only inside continuous sticks and in the chassis grillage (welded, or bolted and squared by the ply).
  • Deck plywood modelled as shear panels (effective G·t lb/in for .75" screwed 6–8" o.c.; APA tables give 60–80k before fastener slip). The skin on the chamfer faces, the belly, the sides and the tail-wall panels beside the door is counted at lb/in. Remove the chamfer panels and the rim becomes a handrail that sways 2" under a tow snatch.
  • Casters are stiff legs from the rails to the road, pinned at the tyre contact. 12 ga section properties from the Unistrut Telespar datasheet (net area at a hole); 14 ga derived from them by the gross-section ratio, so the datasheet's hole knock-down carries over. Fy = ksi; confirm the grade on the 14 ga you actually buy, some generic perforated tube is 33–36 ksi, which would push the chassis back to 12 ga.
  • Capacity: yield (P/A + M/S) and AISC E3 column buckling with K = 1 over the node-to-node length. Static, Sway, Tow, Lifted, Stop are checked against ASD allowables (÷1.67); Shock already carries a 2× factor so it is checked against nominal strength. Cables, if enabled, against breaking strength ÷ 5 (÷ 2.5 under Shock).
  • Not modelled: local wall dimpling under a bolt, hole slop, skin on the cones (helps), wind on a skinned box.
